What to watch for

AI is fast, capable, and sometimes confidently wrong. Five minutes here on what to trust, what to double-check, and what's safe to put in, so it saves you time instead of costing you.

5 min read
Start Here

Treat it like a sharp intern, not an oracle.

The most useful way to hold it: you've just hired a brilliant, impossibly fast intern who has read almost everything and will hand you a confident answer to any question in seconds. That intern is right most of the time and wrong some of the time. Here's the catch that trips people up: it sounds exactly the same either way.

Everything on this page is how you get the upside without getting burned by the downside. It's short on purpose. Learn these three things once and you can stop worrying about the rest.

Used well, it's the best assistant you've ever had. Used blindly, it'll hand you a wrong number inside a beautiful sentence. The whole game is knowing where the edges are.
What it's for

Great at first drafts. Shaky at final facts.

Most of what you'll ask lives in the safe zone. The trouble only starts when you make it the source of truth on something specific and verifiable.

  • Lean on it freelyDrafting and rewriting. Summarizing something long. Explaining an idea in plain terms. Brainstorming options. Turning a mess of notes into structure. First passes of almost anything.
Double-check before you rely on it

Exact figures, dates, and names. Recent events (it may simply not know them). Math and calculations. Quotes and citations. And anything where being confidently wrong costs you real money or real trust.

The one habit

It will never tell you when it's unsure.

Here's the part most people get wrong. When the AI doesn't know something, it doesn't stop or shrug or say "I'm not sure." It fills the gap with the most plausible-sounding answer and hands it over with the same confidence as a fact it's certain of.

That's not lying, and it's not a bug you can turn off. It's how the tool works: it predicts likely-sounding text, and a wrong name or an invented statistic can be very likely-sounding text. The industry calls this a "hallucination." A plainer word is confabulation: filling a blank with something that fits, and believing it.

So the habit is one line: verify anything you'd be embarrassed to be wrong about. Names, numbers, dates, quotes, and any legal, medical, or financial specifics. If it's going in front of a client or a boss, check it.
A ten-second check

Ask it, "how sure are you about this, and what's your source?" It will often flag its own shaky spots when you push. But for anything that matters, confirm against a real source. Don't treat its answer as the source.

What's safe to put in

Treat the message box like an email to an outside vendor.

One rule of thumb keeps you out of almost all the trouble: if you wouldn't put it in an email to a company you don't fully control, don't paste it into a consumer AI tool.

  • Keep these outPasswords, account numbers, and Social Security numbers. Client or patient records you're legally bound to protect. Anything under an NDA. Unreleased financials or live legal matters.
Personal vs. business accounts

On a personal plan, your chats may be used to improve the models unless you switch that off in settings. Business and enterprise plans generally don't train on your data and add real protections. If you'll be using genuine company information, get on the right plan and check the data settings. We cover the account tiers in Getting Set Up.

None of this should scare you off. The vast majority of your work, the drafting and thinking and organizing and explaining, has nothing sensitive in it at all. This is just the short list of what to hold back.

The takeaway

You know what to watch for.

Four things true, and you can move fast without getting burned.

  • I trust it for drafts and thinking, not as the final word on facts
  • I verify names, numbers, dates, and quotes before they leave my hands
  • I keep secrets and protected data out of the message box
  • I'm on the right account for the kind of information I'll use
