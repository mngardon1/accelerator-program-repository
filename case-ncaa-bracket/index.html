The Problem

Analysis I would never actually do

Eight of us run a snake draft of NCAA tournament teams. The scoring rewards survival, not favorites: each team pays its seed times the round it reaches, so a 15-seed that wins a second-round game pays $30 and a 1-seed that wins it pays $2. Everyone senses the strategy, draft middle seeds that go far, but nobody can quantify it across 64 teams.

Here is the honest part: before AI, there is zero chance I do this. Ranking 64 teams by expected value is a weekend of work for a game night, so I never bothered. This time I just tried AI to see what it could do.

Strip away the basketball and this is a portfolio problem: rank 64 options by expected value under uncertainty, then pick in order. The kind of analysis that stays undone because it costs too much time.

The Prompt

Here is everything I typed

The opening message

"I'm doing an 8-team snake draft for the NCAA basketball tournament. The game pays seed × the round, so a 15-seed that wins a second-round game gets $30. The strategy is to pick middle seeds that will go far in the tournament. I'd like you to research all 64 teams and come up with a ranking system to help me draft. I have the 6th pick in the first round."

No formula. No code. I described the game, the scoring rule, my intuition, and my one constraint, the way you would brief a sharp analyst.

If you can explain the rules of the game and what you are trying to win, you can brief the model. That is the whole skill.

What It Built

A model, a ranked board, and a strategy, in minutes

It researched all 64 teams across the four regions, pulled the current bracket and betting odds, and built an expected-value model: a team's value equals its seed times the sum, over each round, of the round number times its odds of getting there. Then it delivered a spreadsheet with three tabs: a full ranked draft board color-coded by tier, my specific snake-draft picks, and a strategy guide.

The favorites are a trap. Duke winning it all pays $21. A 4-seed winning it all pays $84. The money lives in the 4 to 7 seeds.

One pass produced what a sharp analyst might spend a weekend on: a defensible model, a ranked board, and a plan of attack.

The Tweaks

Then I nudged it, one prompt at a time

The first board was good. The edge came from a few plain-English follow-ups, each one sharpening the model.

Tweak 1 · Find the real edge

"What other data sources would we need to swing these rankings? Injury reports? Betting odds and history? Last 10 game streak? Think outside the box to get an edge."

It came back with the edges that actually move money: KenPom efficiency (how a team wins, not just that it does), injury reports (North Carolina had lost its best player, so a 6-seed was really a 10-seed), Vegas round-by-round odds, and tempo, you want your mid-seeds to be high-variance chaos teams, not slow grinders.

Tweak 2 · Challenge the pick

I pushed back on its championship pick. It was riding raw talent and under-weighting a key injury.

It revised in real time: a point-guard injury compounds every round against elite defenses, so Duke bows out in the Final Four instead of cutting down the nets.

Tweak 3 · Go deeper

"I have Florida and Michigan in the final now. Predict the score."

It reasoned through both profiles and called a low-scoring defensive grinder, Michigan 63, Florida 58, detailed enough to sanity-check every branch of the board.

The value was never the first answer. It was interrogating it, the same judgment I would bring to any analyst's model. AI proposes. I still decide.

Why It Matters

This was never about basketball

The whole thing took about 45 minutes, for a game night, and I won the pool. But it was a messy, multi-variable ranking decision made under uncertainty, built and pressure-tested by talking. Now swap the 64 teams for your vendors, your markets, your open deals, your hiring shortlist, or your feature backlog. Same move. The model does the math. You bring the judgment and the pushback.
