Case Study

I got a ranked board for my Little League draft from eight spreadsheets I never opened.

I coach Little League on nights and weekends. The league coordinator handed me five spreadsheets and three Word docs on 156 kids and said good luck. There was no world where I cross-referenced all of that by hand to rank a 13-round draft. So I dropped the files in a folder, described what I wanted, and thirty minutes later I had one ranked board. The only thing I did was drag in the files.

The Problem

Who has time to merge, dedupe, and combine 8 spreadsheets?

Not a volunteer dad coach on a school night. The coordinator sent a master list of 156 kids plus separate coach-evaluation files, a self-rating column, tournament rosters, and seasons played, eight files in all, in different shapes, none of them talking to each other. Everything I needed to draft well was in there, buried.

So the honest outcome, every year, is that you draft on gut and a few names you remember. The analysis just does not happen.

This is the everyday version of the real problem: the data is real, but it is spread across files in inconsistent shapes, so the good decision loses to the fast one.

The Prompt

I pointed it at the folder

The opening message

"I'm doing a draft of 11 and 12 year olds for Little League. In this folder are several spreadsheets. Analyze these by player and consolidate the information so I can rank my choices. Please ask me questions for clarification as we go. Ideally we'd have a separate spreadsheet with all the 11 and 12 year olds in rank order by round. We may have up to 13 rounds."

I formatted nothing. I dropped the coordinator's files into a folder, pointed at it, and said what I wanted, including that it should ask me questions instead of guessing.

If you can hand a sharp assistant a folder and describe the outcome, you can do this. That is the entire ask.

How It Worked

It asked before it built

It opened the folder, found the master list of 156 players and the twelve coach-evaluation files, and then did the thing a good analyst does: it asked how I wanted the decision made before making it. How should it weight coach evals versus tournament experience versus seasons played? What were the different files? How many teams were drafting?

Coach evals first, then tournament experience, then seasons played, positions useful. My weighting. Its math.

I answered in a sentence or two. Twelve teams, thirteen rounds, 156 picks. It consolidated every file into one ranked draft board, in order, by round.

It behaved like a good hire: it learned how I think about the decision before it handed me an answer.

The Tweaks

Then I steered it, one line at a time

The first board was close. A few plain-English nudges made it mine.

Tweak 1 · Scope it

"Starting with the Draft List spreadsheet, only tab 'Major BB', those are the only names I care about."

It trimmed the pool to exactly the players in play, so the board was not cluttered with names I would never draft.

Tweak 2 · Keep my judgment in it

I had it fold my own 32 notes into the board, side by side with its own.

It merged them into a "Your Notes" column next to an "Asst Notes" column, so I could see my read and its read on every kid at a glance. I stayed the decision-maker.

Tweak 3 · Add a new source

"Use this sheet and add the Tournament Team for each kid under 'Travel Team' in the draft board."

I dragged in the travel-team rosters mid-stream. It tagged 34 players with their specific tournament team and flagged their experience, no rebuild required.

Each nudge sharpened the board. It kept my notes next to its own, so the judgment stayed mine.

Why It Matters

This was never about baseball

It took thirty minutes, on a school night, and I walked into the draft with the best board in the room. But the real move was consolidating messy, inconsistent data from a pile of sources into one ranked, decision-ready view. Swap the 156 kids for your candidates, your vendors, your accounts, or your deals. The files are already sitting in a folder somewhere. This is how you finally use them.
